About Bead 3

Bead 3, also known as Three Guti, Three Bead, or Three Men's Morris, is a free online strategy board game from the Raxzen game collection. Inspired by the traditional Three Guti-style board game played across South Asia for generations, Bead 3 combines a simple set of rules with a carefully designed premium tabletop-style interface, smooth interactions, responsive controls, sound effects, and a modern browser-based experience.

The game is designed to be quick to understand but strategic enough to make every move matter. You can challenge the computer or play directly against a friend on the same device — no downloads, no accounts, no hassle.

♟️ How the Game Works

Each player controls three pieces on a connected 3×3 board with diagonal lines. The objective is to move your pieces strategically and create a winning line of three before your opponent does.

The Board

The classic board consists of 9 intersection points arranged in a 3×3 grid, connected by horizontal, vertical, and diagonal lines.

There are 8 possible winning lines: 3 horizontal rows, 3 vertical columns, and 2 diagonals.

The Two Phases of Play

Phase 1 — Placing Pieces (Placement Phase)

  • The board starts empty.
  • Players take turns placing one piece at a time on any empty intersection.
  • Pieces alternate: Player A → Player B → Player A → Player B, and so on.
  • If a player creates a line of three during placement, they win immediately.
  • Once placed, a piece cannot be moved until Phase 2 begins.

Phase 2 — Moving Pieces (Movement Phase)

  • After all 6 pieces (3 per player) are placed on the board, Phase 2 begins.
  • On each turn, a player moves one of their own pieces to an adjacent empty intersection point connected by a line.
  • Pieces can only move along marked lines to directly connected points.
  • Jumping over other pieces or moving to non-adjacent points is not allowed.
  • The first player to form a straight line of three pieces wins.

Winning Condition

The first player to successfully create a valid straight line of three pieces — horizontally, vertically, or diagonally — wins the round.

The game also includes a randomized first turn, so every new round can begin differently, adding variety and replay value.

🤖 Play Against the Computer

Want to play alone? Choose "Vs Computer" and challenge the built-in AI opponent. The computer opponent can:

  • Detect all available legal moves on the board
  • Look for an immediate winning move and take it
  • Block your winning threats when possible
  • Choose strategic moves when no winning move is available
  • Respond automatically during its turn
  • Continue the game without requiring another player

This provides a simple single-player experience while keeping the game lightweight and easy to start.

👥 Play With a Friend

Bead 3 also includes a "Vs Friend" mode, allowing two people to play on the same device. No second account or separate device is required — simply hand the device to the other player and take turns. This makes it ideal for:

  • Offline-style local play
  • Short strategy matches
  • Casual entertainment anywhere

🧠 Simple to Learn, Strategic to Master

The rules are straightforward, but winning requires planning. A good player needs to think about:

  • Where to move and which position to control
  • How to block the opponent's winning lines
  • How to create a winning line of their own
  • How to avoid giving the opponent an easy opportunity
  • How to set up a "fork" — a position where two different winning lines are threatened at once, making it impossible for the opponent to block both

Because each player only has three pieces, every single move can have a meaningful impact on the result. The center of the board is especially valuable because it participates in four different winning lines (horizontal, vertical, and both diagonals), giving the player who controls it a significant strategic advantage.

🔄 Designed for Replayability

Bead 3 is designed for quick rounds, making it easy to play again and again. After a round ends, players can immediately start another game and try a different strategy. You can switch between Vs Computer and Vs Friend modes whenever you want, keeping the experience fresh and engaging.

🎨 Design Language — The 3 Guti Game Board

Bead 3 uses a premium, minimal, dark wooden game-board interface with a wood-and-brass theme — a clean, elegant, game-first design with realistic 3D pieces.

Top status bar: centered player labels "IVORY" and "COMPUTER," each shown with three small pieces representing remaining pieces on the board, set in simple spacing and uppercase typography that clearly shows both players and their progress.

Game board: a tall vertical rounded-rectangle board with a thick double gold/bronze outer border, a dark brown wooden background with a subtle diagonal wood-grain texture, and an inner border that creates a premium framed appearance around a large, uncluttered playing area.

Board layout: 9 playable points arranged in a 3×3 structure — three on the top row, three across the center, three on the bottom row — connected vertically between corresponding columns, horizontally across each row, and diagonally between the corners and the center, which acts as the main intersection point.

Pieces: Ivory pieces are glossy off-white/cream 3D spheres, while the opponent's pieces are glossy dark/black 3D spheres, each rendered with realistic gradients, highlights, shadows, and depth. Small circular gold markers frame the playable points on the board.

Visual palette: dark brown, antique gold, and ivory tones with soft shadows, subtle highlights, and a slight 3D depth effect create high contrast between pieces, board, and lines — with no clutter and no unnecessary buttons or decorations, for an overall feel that is premium, polished, and modern while preserving the traditional board-game identity.

Historical Timeline

  • Ancient period — 3-piece alignment-type games emerge across multiple civilizations.
  • Roman period — Referenced in Ovid's Ars Amatoria (~1st century CE); boards and pieces found at archaeological sites.
  • Medieval Europe — Documented as "Alquerque de tres" in 13th-century Spain (Libro de los juegos); "Three Men's Morris" and "Nine Holes" recorded in England by the 1300s.
  • Regional European variants — Marelle (France), Mühle (Germany), Mølle (Scandinavia), Triodi (Greece).
  • South Asian variants — Tant Fant, Tin Guti, and Tuk-Tak documented in Bengal and surrounding regions.
  • Modern digital era — Online versions like Bead 3 bring the classic experience to browsers worldwide.

A note on origins: some sources have proposed connections to ancient Egyptian boards at Kurna (~1400 BCE), but these claims remain disputed among historians, as do certain Roman and Chinese historical attributions. The most honest answer is that a single point of origin is not definitively known — alignment games of this type appear to have arisen independently, or spread through cultural exchange, across the Mediterranean, Europe, the Middle East, South Asia, Africa, and Southeast Asia over many centuries.

🏆 Winning Strategies & Tips

1

Control the center. The center intersection is the most powerful position on the board because it participates in four winning lines (horizontal, vertical, and both diagonals). Controlling it gives you the most flexibility.

2

Create a fork. Set up your pieces so your next move threatens two different winning lines at once. Your opponent can only block one, guaranteeing you a win on the following turn.

3

Block early. If your opponent has two pieces in a line with an empty third spot, block it immediately — one missed block can end the game.

4

Use corners strategically. Corner pieces can contribute to both a diagonal and a horizontal or vertical line, creating multiple threats later.

5

Avoid traps. Be careful not to move a piece into a position where your opponent can force a fork. Always look two moves ahead when possible.

6

First-move advantage. In some rule variations the first player has a slight mathematical advantage — some versions restrict the first move from the center to balance the game.

🔀 Rule Variations

Different regions and digital implementations use slightly different rules. The two most common variations are:

Variant A — Classic (Adjacent Movement): pieces move only to directly connected adjacent empty points. This is the most widely recognized traditional version, and the one used in Bead 3 and most historical descriptions.

Variant B — Free Movement (Any Empty Point): after placement, pieces can move to any empty point on the board, not just adjacent ones. This is documented by historian H.J.R. Murray as "Nine Holes," and is less common but still valid in some implementations.

Bead 3 uses the Classic Adjacent Movement rules for an authentic traditional experience.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

What is Bead 3?

Bead 3 is a free online version of the traditional Three Men's Morris board game. Two players each control three pieces and compete to form a straight line of three on a 3×3 connected board. You can play against the computer or with a friend on the same device.

Is Bead 3 the same as Tic-Tac-Toe?

Not exactly. While both games involve getting three in a row, Bead 3 has a movement phase where pieces can be moved after placement, making it more dynamic and strategic than standard Tic-Tac-Toe.

How do I win in Bead 3?

You win by being the first player to arrange all three of your pieces in a straight line — horizontally, vertically, or diagonally — on the board.

How old is this game?

Games of this type have existed for many centuries across multiple civilizations. Documented evidence dates back to medieval Europe (13th century) and possibly ancient Rome. The exact origin isn't definitively known, but it's one of humanity's oldest board game families.

What are the different names for this game?

It's known as Three Men's Morris, Three Guti, Tin Guti, Tant Fant, Tapatan, Achi, Shisima, Marelle, Tres en Raya, and many other names across different countries and cultures.

Is 3 Guti the same as 16 Guti?

No. They're completely different games. 3 Guti uses 3 pieces per player on a small board with no capturing. 16 Guti (also called Sholo Guti) uses 16 pieces per player on a larger board with capture-by-jumping mechanics.

Can the game end in a draw?

Yes. If both players' pieces become stuck in positions where no winning line can be formed, the game can end in a draw or tie, depending on the specific rules being used.
